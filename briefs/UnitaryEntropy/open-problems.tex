\noindent
Open problems raised by the technical brief in this directory, and by its Lean formalization
(see \lean{formalization-log.tex}).

\section*{From the brief}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Classical counterpart.} State and prove the classical analogue: Hamiltonian
  evolution is equivalent to preservation of the Shannon entropy and of ``the definitional
  independence of degrees of freedom''. (Brief, Sec.~4: ``This parallels the classical case
  where Hamiltonian evolution is equivalent to the preservation of Shannon entropy (Liouville's
  theorem)''.)
\item \textbf{Entropy conservation as determinism and reversibility.} The brief reads DR-INFO as
  ``an information theoretic way to characterize determinism and reversibility''. A precise
  definition of deterministic and reversible evolution, and a proof that it is equivalent to
  DR-INFO, are not given. (Brief, Sec.~1.)
\item \textbf{Propagator for unbounded time-dependent $H(t)$.} ``The Schr\"odinger equation
  gives rise to a differentiable unitary propagator $U_{t_0\to t_1}$'' is assumed. For a
  continuous family of bounded self-adjoint $H(t)$ this is now proved, in any dimension, from
  the global existence and uniqueness theorem for linear ODEs: the propagator exists, is unique
  and is unitary (\lean{exists_propagator_of_continuous}, \lean{propagator_unique}), and in finite
  dimension its two-parameter evolution satisfies [B]EVMIX, DR-SCEQ, DR-INFO and EVDIFF
  (\lean{TwoParameter.exists_propagator_isEvolution}; log, ``The propagator of a time-dependent
  Hamiltonian''). Existence, uniqueness and unitarity also hold for bounded $H(t)$ that are only
  strongly continuous, with $t \mapsto U(t)\psi$ differentiable for every $\psi$
  (\lean{exists_propagator_of_continuous_apply}), and so does EVDIFF: $U(t)\rho U(t)^\dagger$ is
  differentiable in operator norm for every density operator $\rho$
  (\lean{hasDerivAt_conj_of_continuous_apply}). The propagator itself need not be
  norm-differentiable: on $L^2[0,1]$, multiplication by the indicator of $[0,t]$ is such an
  $H(t)$, and no propagator of it is norm-differentiable on $(0,1)$
  (\lean{indicatorHamiltonian_counterexample}). For unbounded self-adjoint $H(t)$ existence
  needs Kato-type hypotheses and remains open. The converse has the same gap: for unbounded
  $H(t)$, Step~3 gives only a symmetric $H(t_0)$ (next section, first item). (Brief, Sec.~3,
  proof of Theorem~2 ($\Rightarrow$); Log, ``Differences from the brief''.)
\end{enumerate}

\section*{From the formalization}
\noindent Beyond finite dimension the formalization has a part in any dimension; it leaves
open the items ``Unbounded time-dependent Hamiltonians'', ``Equation at every time in infinite
dimension'' and ``Wold--Cooper reduction'' below. Whether a phase choice makes $t\mapsto U(t)$
differentiable in operator norm in infinite dimension is settled negatively: on
$\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, a phase $e^{it}$ on the basis vector $e_{\lceil 1/|t|\rceil+1}$ satisfies
all hypotheses, with EVDIFF in operator norm, but no unitary family inducing it is
norm-differentiable at $0$ (\lean{movingPhase_counterexample}; log, ``No norm-differentiable
propagator'').
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unbounded time-dependent Hamiltonians.} EVDIFF on every density operator forces a
  bounded $H$ (Banach--Steinhaus). With EVDIFF on a dense set of states, Theorem~2 is formalized
  for a time-independent evolution, with unbounded self-adjoint $H$
  (\lean{drSceq_iff_drInfo_and_evDiff}, by Stone's theorem). Without EVTI, Step~3 gives only a
  skew-symmetric $A(t_0)$, and $H(t_0)$ need not be self-adjoint: the propagator of
  $H(t) = H_\beta$ for $t < 0$ and $H_\alpha$ for $t \ge 0$, two self-adjoint extensions of the
  momentum on $L^2(0,1)$, is differentiable at $0$ exactly on the domain of the minimal momentum
  operator, which is not self-adjoint. Open: the hypotheses (Kato-type, or on the evolution)
  under which DR-INFO + EVDIFF gives a self-adjoint $H(t)$. The example is formalized: the vectors
  on which the propagator is differentiable at $0$ form a dense subspace, on which
  $i\hbar\,U'(0)$ is symmetric but not self-adjoint (\lean{switchedHamiltonian_counterexample}).
  This subspace is $H^1_0(0,1)$, the domain of the minimal momentum, and $i\hbar\,U'(0)$ is
  $-i\,d/dx$ on it (\lean{switchedDomain_eq}). The two pieces of $U$ are
  $e^{-itH_\alpha/\hbar}$ and $e^{-itH_\beta/\hbar}$
  (\lean{twistedTranslation_eq_schrodingerPropagator}).
  (Log, ``Unbounded Hamiltonians''.)

  The example uses the two-sided derivative at $t_0 = 0$, and it splits the problem in two.
  Each one-sided derivative alone has a self-adjoint generator. The right derivative of
  $e^{-itH_\alpha/\hbar}\psi$ at $0$ exists exactly for $\psi$ in the domain of $H_\alpha$, and the
  left derivative of $e^{-itH_\beta/\hbar}\psi$ exactly for $\psi$ in the domain of $H_\beta$
  (\lean{exists_hasDerivWithinAt_iff}). The domain of the two-sided generator is the
  intersection of the two, so it is too small; it is a common restriction of $H_\alpha$ and $H_\beta$
  (\lean{switchedHamiltonian_le_momentum_left}, \lean{switchedHamiltonian_le_momentum_right}).
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] \emph{Two-sided derivative}, as in Step~3: DR-INFO + EVDIFF do not give a
    self-adjoint $H(t_0)$. This is settled by the example above.
  \item[(b)] \emph{Forward derivative only.} The evolution $\Phi_{t_0\to t_1}$ is only given
    for $t_0 \le t_1$, so one can define $H(t_0)$ by the right derivative of
    $t \mapsto \Phi_{t_0\to t}$ at $t_0$. The example above says nothing here: its forward
    generator at $0$ is $H_\alpha$, which is self-adjoint. A continuously varying boundary
    phase settles it negatively (\lean{sqrtPhase_counterexample}). Take
    $H(t) = H_{\alpha(t)}$ with $\alpha(t) = \alpha_0 + \sqrt t$. Its propagator is unitary
    and satisfies $U(t_2,t_1)U(t_1,t_0) = U(t_2,t_0)$, and at every $t_0 \ne 0$ the forward
    generator is the self-adjoint $H_{\alpha(t_0)}$. At $t_0 = 0$ the forward domain is the
    dense $H^1_0(0,1)$, and the forward generator is the minimal momentum, symmetric but not
    self-adjoint (\lean{forwardDomain_sqrtPhase},
    \lean{not_isSelfAdjoint_forwardHamiltonian_sqrtPhase}). Hölder exponent $\tfrac12$ is the
    threshold: if $|\alpha(t_0+s) - \alpha(t_0)| = o(\sqrt s)$, the forward generator is
    $H_{\alpha(t_0)}$ (\lean{forwardHamiltonian_eq_momentum}). So what is missing is regularity
    of the evolution in time. At a single time a hypothesis on the evolution alone is
    formalized. The forward generator of any unitary evolution is symmetric, and it is
    self-adjoint iff at $t_0$ the evolution is tangent to a one-parameter unitary group:
    $U(t_0+\tau,t_0)\psi - e^{-i\tau H/\hbar}\psi = o(\tau)$ for a self-adjoint $H$ and every
    $\psi$ in its domain, and then $H = H(t_0)$
    (\lean{isSelfAdjoint_forwardHamiltonian_iff}). In the example no group is tangent at $0$
    (\lean{not_exists_tangent_sqrtPhase}). A condition on the evolution alone that gives an
    essentially self-adjoint forward generator is formalized: a dense subspace $D$ invariant
    under all $U(t,t_0)$, on which the orbits are differentiable and the generators near $t_0$
    differ from the one at $t_0$ by operators bounded on $D$
    (\lean{isEssentiallySelfAdjoint_forwardHamiltonian}). If the differences also tend to $0$
    as $t \to t_0^+$, the forward generator itself is self-adjoint, by a Duhamel estimate
    (\lean{isSelfAdjoint_forwardHamiltonian}). The example has no such $D$. Open: a relative
    bound in place of a bounded difference, as for a time-dependent unbounded potential. The usual meaning of ``$U$ solves the
    Cauchy problem'' is not enough: dense subspaces $Y_t$ with $U(t,s)Y_s \subseteq Y_t$ on
    which $t \mapsto U(t,s)\psi$ is $C^1$ (Engel--Nagel, Def.~VI.9.2). In the example
    $Y_0 = H^1_0(0,1)$ and $Y_t = D(H_{\alpha(t)})$ for $t > 0$ are such subspaces, by direct
    computation (not formalized): for $\psi \in H^1_0$, $|\psi(1-u)| = o(\sqrt u)$ compensates the
    derivative $\tfrac1{2\sqrt u}$ of the phase. Engel and Nagel note that no analogue of the
    generation theorem is known for time-dependent problems (Sec.~VI.9.a). Kato's theorem gives
    the $\Leftarrow$ direction under its hypotheses (constant domain, $t\mapsto H(t)\psi$ of
    class $C^1$); in the example the domain is not constant.
  \end{enumerate}
  What holds without hypotheses on $H(t)$ is Howland's construction: a strongly continuous
  unitary propagator $U(t,s)$ gives the strongly continuous unitary group
  $(W(\sigma)f)(t) = U(t,t-\sigma)f(t-\sigma)$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R};E)$, hence a self-adjoint
  quasi-energy operator $K$ with $W(\sigma) = e^{-i\sigma K/\hbar}$
  (\lean{UnitaryPropagator.exists_howlandGenerator}). Open: how $K$, formally
  $-i\hbar\,\partial_t + H(t)$, determines $H(t_0)$ at a single time, and whether that
  $H(t_0)$ is self-adjoint.
\item \textbf{Equation at every time in infinite dimension.} In finite dimension, one
  differentiable unitary $U(t)$ for all times solves the Schr\"odinger equation at every time,
  and $\Phi_{t_0\to t_1}$ is conjugation by $U(t_1)U(t_0)^\dagger$
  (\lean{TwoParameter.drSceq_iff_drInfo_and_evDiff}). The proof fixes the determinant of the local
  unitaries, which leaves only a root of unity to match when gluing them. In any dimension the
  determinant is not defined, and the local unitaries are only strongly differentiable, and in
  general cannot be chosen norm-differentiable (\lean{movingPhase_counterexample}), so the global
  propagator is not built there. (Log, ``The equation at every time''.)
\item \textbf{Wold--Cooper reduction.} The right shifts $V_t$ on $L^2(0,\infty)$ give an
  evolution that satisfies [B]EVMIX, maps pure states to pure states, preserves transition
  probabilities and the entropy, but is induced by no unitary; this is formalized
  (\lean{shift_semigroup_counterexample}, in the brief ``Multiple equivalent characterizations of
  the Schr\"odinger equation''). The Wold--Cooper decomposition, which reduces every strongly
  continuous semigroup of isometries to a unitary group plus copies of this one, is not
  formalized. (Log, any-dimension part, ``Problems found in the brief'', second item.)
\end{enumerate}
