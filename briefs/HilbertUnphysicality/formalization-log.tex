\paragraph{Setting.} Theorem~5 says that three physical conditions are not guaranteed over
the Hilbert-space formulation QSTO:H. The brief proves four mathematical propositions (Sec.~2) and
then argues each part of Theorem~5 from one of them (Sec.~4). The propositions are checked in
Lean, and Theorem~5 is stated and proved as \lean{theorem5}, with each condition in its
minimum form (see ``Differences''). Of the additional remarks of
Sec.~5, the one on the domain of the Schr\"odinger equation is checked too, for every
self-adjoint Hamiltonian and in detail for the harmonic oscillator, in the number basis and in
position space, and for the free particle in position space, and so are the
ones on commutator relations, for position and momentum on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, and on
point-wise values, in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ (see ``Differences''). The closing remark of the proof
of Theorem~5, that Schwartz spaces present the same problem, is checked as well, and so are
its remarks that classical mechanics allows a cutoff on all variables and quantum mechanics
does not, in the readings of ``Differences''.

\paragraph{Differences from the brief.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Proposition~1.} Both parts are stated for linear maps, without Mathlib's partially
  defined operators. Discontinuity is proved for an unbounded linear map on any normed
  space, which includes $\mathcal{D}(A)$ with the norm inherited from $\mathcal{H}$.
  Hellinger--Toeplitz is proved for an everywhere-defined symmetric map; self-adjointness is
  not needed.
\item \emph{Proposition~2.} The number operator \lean{numberOp} is the diagonal operator
  with entries $n$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, on its maximal domain, as a partially defined
  operator (\lean{LinearPMap}); its domain is \lean{numberDomain}
  $= \{x : \sum_n n^2|x_n|^2 < \infty\}$. Self-adjointness is proved for every diagonal
  operator with real entries (\lean{lp.diagonal}, in \lean{ForMathlib}). The expectation
  \lean{expN}~$x = \sum_n n\,|x_n|^2 / \|x\|^2$ is defined on all of $\ell^2$ and equals
  $\operatorname{Re}\braket{x|Nx}/\|x\|^2$ on $\mathcal{D}(N)$ (\lean{expN_eq_inner}).
  Discontinuity is proved within $\mathcal{D}(N)\setminus\{0\}$ with the subspace topology,
  as in the minimum form of [B]COBS. The sequence $x_j$ is the brief's.
\item \emph{Proposition~3.} The wave functions are $\psi = (e^{-x^2}/\sqrt\pi)^{1/2}$
  (\lean{gaussState}) and $\phi = (1/(\pi(1+y^2)))^{1/2}$, as elements of
  $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. Both frames use the same space $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ with Lebesgue measure.
  The position operators are both multiplication by the coordinate, \lean{positionOp}, on
  their common maximal domain \lean{positionDomain} $= \{\psi : x\psi \in L^2\}$. Their
  self-adjointness is proved for every real measurable multiplication operator on $L^2(\mu)$
  (\lean{L2.isSelfAdjoint_mulOp}, in \lean{ForMathlib}), by cutting off to the sets
  $\{|a| \le n\}$. That $f$ is strictly increasing and onto
  $\mathbb{R}$, and that $g = f^{-1}$ has $g' > 0$, are proved; smoothness is not, only
  differentiability, which is all the unitary needs. The unitary $U$ is
  \lean{Lp.compDiffeo}, proved unitary for any bijection $g$ of $\mathbb{R}$ that is
  differentiable with a differentiable inverse, with weight $\sqrt{|g'|}$; its inverse $V$ is
  the same construction for $g^{-1}$. The operator $UXU^{-1}$ is the conjugate
  \lean{conjLinearPMap}, with domain $U\,\mathcal{D}(X)$ by definition; that it is
  multiplication by $g(y)$ follows from $U(a\psi) = (a \circ g)\,U\psi$ for every measurable
  $a$ (\lean{Lp.compDiffeo_mulOp}). It differs from $Y$ because $U\psi \in U\,\mathcal{D}(X)$
  but $U\psi \notin \mathcal{D}(Y)$. Mathlib has no error function; \lean{Real.erf} is defined in
  \lean{ForMathlib}.
\item \emph{Proposition~4.} The classification of separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
  is new in \lean{ForMathlib}. The positive integers $n, m$ appear as $n + 1, m + 1$.
\item \emph{Theorem~5.} Stated as \lean{theorem5}, a conjunction of three parts, one per
  condition, each for one particular system: the oscillator (Proposition~2), one spatial
  degree of freedom (Proposition~3), and any two numbers $n+1$, $m+1$ of continuous degrees
  of freedom (Proposition~4). ``Not guaranteed'' (``inconsistent'' in the previous version of
  the brief) is read as the failure of the minimum form of the condition for that system. For
  [B]STDIST, which has no minimum form in the current brief, it is the one of the previous
  version, as the proof uses it. The [B]COBS part states that $N$ is self-adjoint on
  $\mathcal{D}(N)$, that $E_N(x) = \operatorname{Re}\braket{x|Nx}/\|x\|^2$ there, and that
  $E_N$ is not continuous on $\mathcal{D}(N)\setminus\{0\}$. The [B]WDQ part states
  $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(X)$, $U\psi = \phi$ a.e.\ and $U\psi \notin \mathcal{D}(Y)$, for the
  specific unitary $U$ of the change of frame (for an arbitrary unitary the statement would
  be trivial). The [B]STDIST part states that a unitary $L^2(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}) \to
  L^2(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})$ exists and carries the QSTO:H model. For [B]STDIST, that a
  unitary carries rays, density operators, self-adjoint operators, the Born rule and the
  entropy is proved for any unitary between complex Hilbert spaces (\lean{carriesQSTO}).
  Rays are elements of \lean{Projectivization}; density operators and the entropy are those
  of \lean{ForPhyslib} (positive, trace class, trace one; entropy in $[0,\infty]$);
  self-adjoint operators are partially defined (\lean{LinearPMap}).
\item \emph{Theorem~5, Schwartz spaces.} ``Schwartz spaces present the same problem'' is read
  in its strongest form: for all $n, m \ge 1$ there is a unitary $U\colon L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)
  \to L^2(\mathbb{R}^m)$ that carries the QSTO:H model and maps $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n)$
  onto $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^m)$, as an isomorphism of topological vector spaces
  (\lean{schwartz_stdist}). So the rigged Hilbert spaces $\mathcal{S} \subseteq L^2$ do not
  tell the systems apart either. As for [B]STDIST, $U$ does not carry the positions and
  momenta. The proof is the Hermite characterization of $\mathcal{S}$ (the
  $N$-representation of Reed--Simon~I, Sec.~V.3 and its appendix): $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a Schwartz function exactly when
  $\sup_\alpha (1 + |\alpha|)^K |c_\alpha(f)| < \infty$ for all $K$, and these suprema
  define the topology of $\mathcal{S}$. One direction uses the operator
  $\sum_i (x_i + \partial_i)(x_i - \partial_i) + 2$, which multiplies $c_\alpha$ by
  $2(|\alpha| + 1 + n)$, the other the Sobolev bound for the values, Parseval's identity and
  the ladder relations. The unitary $U$ relabels the Hermite functions,
  $h_\alpha \mapsto h_{\sigma(\alpha)}$, by a bijection $\sigma\colon \mathbb{N}^n \to
  \mathbb{N}^m$ built from iterated Cantor pairings, for which $1 + |\sigma(\alpha)|$ and
  $1 + |\alpha|$ are polynomially bounded by each other.
\item \emph{Theorem~5, classical cutoffs.} The brief contrasts [B]COBS and [B]WDQ with
  classical mechanics, where ``one can, in principle, put a cutoff on all variables'' and work
  with ``joint probability distributions with compact support, which bound all moments'', while
  ``Quantum mechanics does not allow that''. Classically the states are read as the probability
  measures on phase space $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n$ concentrated on a compact set
  $K$, with the topology of weak convergence. Every continuous observable then has a finite
  expectation, continuous in the state (\lean{classical_cutoff_cobs}): it agrees on $K$ with a
  bounded continuous function, obtained by clamping. A continuous change of frame $\varphi$
  maps such a state to one concentrated on the compact set $\varphi(K)$, so all moments stay
  finite (\lean{classical_cutoff_wdq}). In quantum mechanics a cutoff on all variables cuts off
  position and momentum, and the only such state is zero (\lean{quantum_no_cutoff}, for one
  degree of freedom): if $\psi \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ vanishes outside $(a, b]$, then
  $G(z) = \int_a^b e^{-2\pi i x z} \psi(x)\,dx$ is entire and agrees with $\mathcal{F}\psi$ on
  $\mathbb{R}$; if $\mathcal{F}\psi$ also vanishes outside a bounded set, the identity theorem
  gives $G = 0$, so all Fourier coefficients of $\psi$ on $[a, b]$ vanish, and $\psi = 0$ by
  Parseval's identity. A cutoff on position alone is allowed, and is not formalized: smooth
  compactly supported states have finite moments of all orders in position and momentum.
\item \emph{Sec.~5, the domain of the Schr\"odinger equation.} The brief says that the
  equation is valid only on $\mathcal{D}(H)$. This is read dynamically: the evolution
  $e^{-itH}$ is defined on all of $\mathcal{H}$, but its orbit $t \mapsto e^{-itH}\psi$
  satisfies $i\,\tfrac{d}{dt}\psi(t) = H\psi(t)$ exactly when $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(H)$, and is
  differentiable at no time otherwise. It is proved for every self-adjoint $H$ that is
  unbounded on its domain (\lean{schrodinger_only_on_domain}): $\mathcal{D}(H) \ne \mathcal{H}$
  by Hellinger--Toeplitz, and $e^{-itH/\hbar}$ is the evolution of Stone's theorem
  (\lean{schrodingerPropagator}, in \lean{ForPhyslib}, from PhyslibAlpha's spectral theorem for
  unbounded self-adjoint operators), whose orbits are differentiable exactly when they start in
  $\mathcal{D}(H)$ (\lean{differentiableAt_schrodingerPropagator_iff}, new in
  \lean{ForPhyslib}). This covers every potential $V$ for which $P^2/2m + V$ is self-adjoint;
  that it is, is proved here for $V = 0$, for bounded measurable $V$, for $V \in L^2 + L^\infty$,
  and for the oscillator, $V(x) = x^2/2$; for every other real potential in
  $L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}$ off a closed null set, it has self-adjoint realizations. The evolution
  is also built explicitly, and
  shown to be the one of Stone's theorem, for the harmonic oscillator in the number
  basis, $H = N$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, in units with $\hbar\omega = 1$ and without the
  zero-point energy, which only adds a global phase. The explicit evolution and the statement
  are proved for every real diagonal operator on $\ell^2$ (\lean{lp.diagonalEvolution}, in
  \lean{ForMathlib}), and for every real multiplication operator on $L^2(\mu)$
  (\lean{L2.mulEvolution}). In position
  space, the momentum operator is $P = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(2\pi\xi)\mathcal{F}$ with Mathlib's
  unitary Fourier transform on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, whose convention
  $\hat\psi(\xi) = \int e^{-2\pi i x\xi}\psi(x)\,dx$ gives the factor $2\pi$ ($\hbar = 1$).
  Its maximal domain is identified with $H^1(\mathbb{R})$ in Mathlib's definition
  (\lean{MemSobolev}~1~2, through the Bessel potential); that this is the space of $L^2$
  functions whose distributional derivative is in $L^2$ is not proved here. The state outside
  $\mathcal{D}(P)$ is $\mathcal{F}^{-1}\phi$, for the Cauchy packet $\phi$ of
  Proposition~3. The Schr\"odinger equation is checked in position space for the free
  particle, $H_0 = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(2\pi^2\xi^2)\mathcal{F}$ (mass~1); that $H_0 = P^2/2$ as
  partially defined operators is not proved. The harmonic oscillator in position space
  ($m = \omega = \hbar = 1$) is $H = \sum_n (n + \tfrac12)\ket{\psi_n}\bra{\psi_n}$ on its
  maximal domain, with TauCeti's Hermite functions $\psi_n$ and their Hilbert basis of
  $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. It is tied to the differential expression $\tfrac12(-\psi'' + x^2\psi)$
  by $H\psi_n = \tfrac12(-\psi_n'' + x^2\psi_n)$, and by uniqueness: $H$ is the only
  self-adjoint operator with these eigenvectors and eigenvalues (\lean{eq_oscillatorHamiltonian},
  from \lean{lp.eq_diagonal}, new in \lean{ForMathlib}). It is also the operator sum
  $H = H_0 + V$ of partially defined operators, with $V = X^2/2$ multiplication by $x^2/2$,
  on $\mathcal{D}(H) = \mathcal{D}(H_0) \cap \mathcal{D}(V)$
  (\lean{oscillatorHamiltonian_eq_add}). The proof computes $H_0$ on Schwartz functions in
  $\mathcal{S}'$, and $V\psi_n$ from the position ladder relation; for $x \in \mathcal{D}(H)$
  the series $\sum_n c_n V\psi_n$ then converges, so $x \in \mathcal{D}(V)$ and
  $x \in \mathcal{D}(H_0)$ because both operators are closed. Hence $H \subseteq H_0 + V$, and
  equality holds because $H_0 + V$ is symmetric and $H$ is self-adjoint
  (\lean{LinearPMap.eq_of_le_of_isFormalAdjoint}, new in \lean{ForMathlib}). In particular
  $H\varphi = -\tfrac12\varphi'' + \tfrac12 x^2\varphi$ for every Schwartz function $\varphi$
  (\lean{oscillatorHamiltonian_toLp}). For a measurable $V$ with $|V| \le C$ almost
  everywhere, $H_0 + V$ is self-adjoint on $\mathcal{D}(H_0)$
  (\lean{isSelfAdjoint_schrodingerHamiltonian}). This is the bounded case of the Kato--Rellich
  theorem, proved directly: multiplication by $V$ is defined on all of $L^2$ and symmetric, and
  a self-adjoint operator plus an everywhere defined symmetric one is self-adjoint
  (\lean{IsSelfAdjoint.add_of_domain_eq_top}, new in \lean{ForMathlib}). Since $H_0$ is unbounded on its domain
  (\lean{exists_lt_norm_freeHamiltonian}), so is $H_0 + V$, and the remark holds for it
  (\lean{bounded_schrodinger_only_on_domain}).

  More generally, let $|V| \le |W| + C$ almost everywhere with $W \in L^2$, that is
  $V \in L^2 + L^\infty$; this includes singular potentials such as $|x|^{-\alpha}$ near $0$ for
  $\alpha < \tfrac12$. Then $H_0 + V$ is self-adjoint on $\mathcal{D}(H_0)$
  (\lean{isSelfAdjoint_schrodingerHamiltonian_of_memLp}), by the Kato--Rellich theorem. The
  relative bound comes from the one-dimensional Sobolev bound: for $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(H_0)$,
  $f = \mathcal{F}\psi$ and $\xi^2 f$ are in $L^2$, so by Cauchy--Schwarz with
  $(\lambda + \xi^2)^{-1} \in L^2$, $\int|f| \le \sqrt{\pi/\lambda}\,(\lambda\|f\|_2 +
  \|\xi^2 f\|_2)$ for $\lambda \ge 1$ (\lean{Lp.integral_norm_le_of_ae_eq_sq_mul}). Mathlib's
  $L^2$ Fourier transform is defined by extension from Schwartz functions; that on an
  integrable $f$ the inverse transform is the Fourier integral is new
  (\lean{Lp.fourierInv_coeFn_eq_of_integrable}, through tempered distributions and the
  uniqueness of distributions given by locally integrable functions). Hence
  $|\psi| \le \int|f| \le \varepsilon\|H_0\psi\| + C_\varepsilon\|\psi\|$ almost everywhere,
  and $\|V\psi\| \le \|W\|_2\,\|\psi\|_\infty + C\|\psi\| \le a\|\psi\| + \tfrac12\|H_0\psi\|$.
  The remark of Sec.~5 follows as for bounded $V$ (\lean{memLp_schrodinger_only_on_domain}).

  TauCeti has the Kato--Rellich theorem (\lean{IsSelfAdjoint.add_of_isRelativelyBounded}), but
  its module cannot be imported together with Physlib's unbounded operators, which Stone's
  theorem here uses: both declare \lean{LinearPMap.adjoint_smul}. The theorem is reached through
  \lean{ForMathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/KatoRellich.lean}, which restates it with
  Mathlib's notions only (\lean{IsSelfAdjoint.add_of_relativeBound}) and imports TauCeti
  privately; modules importing it do not load TauCeti. Files that load private imports, such as
  non-module files and the exporter of \texttt{lake comparator}, still see the clash, so these
  results are in the separate module \lean{HilbertUnphysicality.KatoRellich}, which the
  comparator lane does not import.

  For potentials outside $L^2 + L^\infty$, such as $|x|^\beta$ with $\beta > 0$, $-x^4$ or
  $\pm 1/x^2$, $P^2/2m + V$ is not $H_0$-bounded, and need not be self-adjoint on
  $\mathcal{D}(H_0) \cap \mathcal{D}(V)$ nor have a unique self-adjoint extension. The brief
  speaks of ``the'' Hamiltonian; it is read as any self-adjoint realization: a self-adjoint $H$
  extending $H_0 + V$ on $\mathcal{D}(H_0) \cap \mathcal{D}(V)$. Every real $V$ that is
  square integrable near each point of an open set $U$ with null complement has one
  (\lean{exists_isSelfAdjoint_le_schrodingerHamiltonian}), and the remark holds for every one
  (\lean{realPotential_schrodinger_only_on_domain}); this includes every continuous $V$ and
  every measurable $V$ continuous off $0$. The domain $\mathcal{D}(H_0) \cap \mathcal{D}(V)$
  is dense, since a vector orthogonal to it is orthogonal to every smooth function with compact
  support in $U$, and so vanishes on $U$. The operator commutes with complex conjugation:
  multiplication by a real $V$ does, and so does $H_0$, the Fourier transform of
  multiplication by the even function $2\pi^2\xi^2$, because
  $\mathcal{F}\bar f(\xi) = \overline{\mathcal{F}f(-\xi)}$ (\lean{Lp.fourier_conjugate}, new,
  by density of Schwartz functions). By von Neumann's theorem it has a self-adjoint
  extension; the theorem is new in \lean{ForMathlib}
  (\lean{LinearPMap.exists_isSelfAdjoint_le_of_commutesWithConj}). The proof takes, by Zorn's
  lemma, a maximal symmetric extension $S$ commuting with the conjugation $C$. It is closed, as
  $S^{**}$ is another one. If $S^*k = ik$ with $k \ne 0$, then $S^*Ck = -iCk$, and $S$ extends
  to $\mathcal{D}(S) + \mathrm{span}(k + Ck)$, which is excluded; so the deficiency spaces are
  zero, and $S$ is self-adjoint by TauCeti's criterion, reached through a private import as
  for Kato--Rellich (\lean{isSelfAdjoint_of_surjective_addSub}). The extension is unbounded,
  since already $H_0 + V$ is
  (\lean{exists_lt_norm_schrodingerHamiltonian_of_locallyIntegrableOn}): for a bump $f$ with
  support in $U$, multiplying by $e^{itx}$ changes neither $\|f\|$ nor $\|Vf\|$, while
  \[ \|H_0\,e^{itx}f\| \ge \tfrac12\,\bigl(t^2\|f\| - 2t\|f'\| - \|f''\|\bigr). \]
  The self-adjoint
  realization is not unique in general: for $-x^4$ or $-1/x^2$ they differ by boundary
  conditions at infinity or at $0$. Which one is physical is a choice of the model, not a
  condition of the remark, which holds for each. For the potential part, $V(y) = y^2$ and the
  state $\phi = U\psi$ of Proposition~3 are used: $\phi \in L^2$, but $V\phi \notin L^2$; for
  $V(y) = y$ this is \lean{frameUnitary_gaussState_not_mem}.
\item \emph{Sec.~5, commutator relations.} The brief says that $[X,P]/(i\hbar) = I$ cannot
  hold on the whole of $\mathcal{H}$, since the left side is unbounded, and must be restricted
  to a domain. Both halves are proved, with $\hbar = 1$. The commutator \lean{commutatorXP} is
  $XP - PX$ on its natural domain $\{\psi \in \mathcal{D}(X) \cap \mathcal{D}(P) : P\psi \in
  \mathcal{D}(X),\ X\psi \in \mathcal{D}(P)\}$. Mathlib composes partially defined operators
  only when the range of one lies in the domain of the other, so the composition on the
  natural domain is new in \lean{ForMathlib} (\lean{LinearPMap.compMax}). That the relation
  cannot hold everywhere is proved in two forms: $\mathcal{D}([X,P]) \ne L^2(\mathbb{R})$;
  and, on any nonzero Hilbert space, no everywhere-defined symmetric $X$, $P$ satisfy
  $XP - PX = i\hbar I$. The second form is the Wielandt--Wintner theorem (in a nontrivial
  normed algebra no commutator is a nonzero scalar, \lean{commutator_ne_algebraMap}, in
  \lean{ForMathlib}) combined with Hellinger--Toeplitz. That the restricted relation holds is
  proved on all of $\mathcal{D}([X,P])$: in the tempered distributions, $X$ is
  multiplication by $x$ and $P = -i\,d/dx$, the Leibniz rule
  $\tfrac{d}{dx}(xu) = u + x\tfrac{du}{dx}$ holds
  (\lean{TemperedDistribution.lineDerivOp_smulLeftCLM}, in \lean{ForMathlib}), and the
  embedding $L^2 \to \mathcal{S}'$ is injective. Schwartz functions lie in
  $\mathcal{D}([X,P])$, so it is dense. Closures and self-adjoint extensions are not
  considered.
\item \emph{Sec.~5, point-wise values.} The brief says that a vector of $L^2$ is a class of
  functions that agree up to a null set, so its values at a point, and the density
  $|\psi(x)|^2$, are not uniquely defined. This is proved for $L^2(\mathbb{R})$: since points
  are null, changing a representative at one point gives another representative
  (\lean{MeasureTheory.update_ae_eq}, in \lean{ForMathlib}), so every value at every point is
  taken, by $\psi$ and by $|\psi|^2$. Beyond the brief, the value at a point cannot be fixed
  by a continuous choice either: no continuous linear functional on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ agrees
  with $\varphi \mapsto \varphi(x_0)$ on the Schwartz functions
  (\lean{not_exists_continuous_eval}). The witnesses are smooth bumps of value $1$ at $x_0$
  whose $L^2$ norm tends to $0$. That $|\psi(x)|^2$ is ``conceptually uniquely defined'' is the
  physical intuition the brief contrasts with, and is not a mathematical claim.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Problems found in the brief.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Cross-references lack their label: the proof of Theorem~5 has ``This satisfies the
  conditions for 2'', ``As shown in 3'' and ``By 4'' for Propositions~2, 3 and~4.
\item ``Schwartz spaces present the same problem'' is asserted without proof, and without
  saying which structure on $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is meant. The formalization proves it
  in its strongest form (see ``Differences'').
\item QSTO:H still allows any separable Hilbert space, finite-dimensional ones included. This
  does not affect Theorem~5, which only claims that the conditions are not guaranteed, that is,
  that each fails for some system.
\item The current brief states [B]STDIST as ``Systems with a different number of degrees of
  freedom are distinct'', which does not say in what sense they are distinct. The minimum form
  of the previous version, that they ``should not have the same mathematical
  representation'', was dropped, but the proof of Theorem~5 still concludes that they have
  ``the same mathematical representation''. The formalization keeps that minimum form.
\item The remarks on classical cutoffs in the proof of Theorem~5 are informal: neither the
  classical states with a cutoff nor the sense in which quantum mechanics does not allow one
  is specified. The formalization proves both in the readings given in
  ``Differences''.
\end{itemize}
